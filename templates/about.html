About the platform

How AI Vision 2035 works

This page documents the whole pipeline: what the dataset contains, how it was cleaned, which models were trained, how the forecast is constructed, and — importantly — what the data cannot tell you.

Project overview

AI Vision 2035 is a Flask analytics application built over a single dataset of 6,000 company-year records covering AI adoption between 2020 and 2025. It has two jobs. The first is descriptive: summarise what the six years of history actually show across companies, countries, industries, revenue bands and use cases. The second is predictive: train a regression model on that history and use it to project an AI maturity score for any profile out to 2035.

Every number rendered in the browser is computed server-side from dataset.csv when the page requests it. No statistic is hard-coded into a template, and no external service is called at any point.

Objectives

  • Quantify how AI adoption relates to measured AI maturity across the 2020–2025 window.
  • Compare maturity across countries, industries and companies while flagging segments too small to rank.
  • Train and benchmark three regression models on identical splits and select the strongest on held-out data.
  • Project AI maturity to 2035 under explicit, user-selectable scenarios.
  • Turn each prediction into a recommendation tied to the predicted maturity band.
  • Present all of it in an interface that states its own assumptions rather than hiding them.

Source file: ai-adoption-fortune500-synthetic-dataset-2020-2025.csv — 6,000 rows, 11 columns, one row per company per year. 1,000 companies each appear in all six years.

Features and target

The model predicts AI_Maturity_Score from eight input features. Company is deliberately excluded: with 1,000 identities and six rows each, one-hot encoding it would let the model memorise individual firms rather than learn transferable structure, and it would be useless for any company not already in the file.

Historical analysis

Section 1 of the dashboard aggregates the file eight ways: by year, by country, by industry, by company, revenue against maturity, ROI band, use-case mix, and adoption growth. Aggregations run in Pandas and are returned as JSON; the browser draws them as SVG.

The clearest finding is the adoption gap. Companies with an active AI use case average 62.8 on maturity against 19.7 for those without — a 43-point difference. Nothing else in the dataset produces an effect anywhere near that size: the full spread between the best and worst well-sampled country is under three points.

Any segment holding fewer than 60 records is drawn in amber and labelled in its tooltip, and is left out of the generated findings entirely.

Why selection is not on R² alone

The three models land within 0.014 R² of each other — a gap smaller than the spread of five-fold cross-validation on this data. Picking the top number would be selecting on noise.

It also matters that this model is used twice: once to score a single profile, and again to trace a ten-year forecast curve. The second use needs a smooth, non-decreasing response to a rising input. Tree ensembles cannot provide that, because they are piecewise-constant by construction. Tuned with small leaves the Random Forest produced a visibly jagged curve; tuned with large leaves it produced a flat line with sudden cliffs. Neither is a defensible forecast.

So the selection rule has two stages, and stays fully automatic. Any model within R2_TOLERANCE = 0.02 of the best score is treated as accuracy-equivalent, and the tie is broken on a measured response stability score. That score sweeps AI_ROI_Percent across its observed range for 16 real profiles and records two things: the share of steps that do not move backwards, and the share of sweep points producing a distinct value. Both numbers are reported in the table above.

On this dataset all three models qualify on accuracy, and Linear Regression wins the tie-break at 0.92 against 0.66 for the Random Forest and 0.51 for the Decision Tree. Trading 0.014 R² for a forecast curve that behaves sensibly is the right exchange here. Re-run train_model.py on different data and the rule may well pick a different model — nothing is hard-coded.

Future forecasting

The model maps features to a maturity score; it does not extrapolate time on its own. To forecast a future year, the input features must be projected forward first, then passed through the saved pipeline. That projection is where the assumptions live, so the dashboard makes you choose one.

  • Data trend — rolls ROI and revenue forward on the slope measured in the selected segment's own history. Because that history is flat, this produces a near-flat forecast. It is the honest baseline.
  • Moderate adoption — assumes AI ROI improves 1.5 points and revenue grows 4% per year.
  • Accelerated adoption — assumes 3.5 points of ROI improvement and 8% revenue growth per year.

The last two are stated assumptions, not measurements, and the dashboard labels them as such next to every result. Projected ROI is clipped to the range observed in the data so the model is never asked to score an input it has never seen, and predictions are clipped to 0–100.

Growth is reported against a 2025 baseline scored by the same model on the same profile, so the comparison isolates the effect of the projected features rather than mixing model error into the difference.

Technologies used

HTML5 CSS3 JavaScript (ES6) Python 3 Flask Pandas NumPy scikit-learn Joblib

No React, no Bootstrap, no Tailwind, no Node, no Django, no database and no third-party API. The chart engine in static/js/charts.js is roughly 300 lines of hand-written SVG drawing code covering line, area, bar, scatter and pie, with tooltips and resize handling. Layout, glass effects and animation are plain CSS.

Future scope

  • Real longitudinal data. The single biggest improvement would be a source where company attributes stay stable year to year, which would make genuine per-company trend modelling possible.
  • Prediction intervals. Quantile regression or a bootstrap over the forest would let each forecast carry a range rather than a point estimate.
  • Explainability. Per-prediction feature attributions would show which inputs drove a given score.
  • Time-aware validation. Training on 2020–2023 and testing on 2024–2025 would measure forward generalisation properly, rather than a random split.
  • Scenario comparison. Running all three scenarios side by side on one chart.
  • Export. Downloading a forecast and its assumptions as CSV or PDF.
